The <a href='https://www.doctorswithoutborders.org/latest/prevention-should-not-be-privilege-msf-calls-gilead-direct-access-lenacapavir' target=_blank>recent exchange between Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) and Gilead</a> on access to lenacapavir (LEN) has drawn attention to how global health systems are evolving. At first glance, the issue appears to be about supply constraints and procurement pathways. However, it raises deeper questions about the direction of global supply chains. Are we moving toward increasingly centralized procurement models, where access to essential, life-saving commodities is concentrated through a limited number of purchasers? At the same time, it invites a broader reflection: how do we pursue supply chain integration both at local and global levels without losing the diversity of actors needed to deliver equitable health outcomes?

At the heart of the issue is access. MSF sought to procure LEN directly from Gilead, aiming to reach populations that are often outside government-led programs, including those in fragile settings, informal settlements, or facing stigma and criminalization. Gilead has been channeling supply through Global Fund procurement mechanisms, with PEPFAR also operating within this structure. This approach supports coordination and scale, while also raising questions about flexibility and reach. Access is currently linked to where procurement decisions have been made, and as a result, some countries may not yet be part of early access pathways. This includes countries with significant HIV burden. For example, countries such as Tanzania, Angola, and South Sudan continue to face substantial HIV incidence among inadequately served populations but are not yet clearly positioned within initial LEN access pathways. Together, these countries represent a meaningful share of the global HIV burden, with hundreds of thousands of new infections occurring annually across similar settings, underscoring the importance of inclusive access planning.

Integration has long been a goal in global health. It seeks to reduce duplication, strengthen country ownership, and improve efficiency; important and necessary objectives. As systems become more aligned and centralized, however, care is needed. Integration should not narrow the pathways through which services are delivered, especially for those most at risk. Equity in this context means making deliberate efforts to reach all populations and geographies, not only those easiest to serve within existing system boundaries and mainstreamed modalities.

From villages to the global level, community actors play a critical role in bridging gaps that formal systems often struggle to reach. Community-led organizations, peer networks, and independent implementers have long been at the forefront, reaching inadequately served populations, innovating service delivery, and strengthening accountability. Their strength lies in trust, proximity, and adaptability. In many settings, community-led services remain the main entry point for underserved populations, highlighting the need to sustain these channels alongside integrated approaches.

The MSF–Gilead situation brings out an important tension. When access to new technologies is structured mainly through centralized procurement and public sector delivery, some populations may be left out. This does not mean integration is the problem. Rather, it suggests that integration needs to be designed carefully. <a href='https://avac.org/blog/global-health-watch-issue62/' target=_blank>According to AVAC’s recent Global Healthwatch</a>, current procurement levels also illustrate the challenge. Around 600,000–700,000 person-years of LEN have been ordered for 2026 for lower-middle income countries (LMICs), which is lower than the stated potential manufacturing capacity and volumes that Gilead had earlier indicated. This gap suggests that current volumes may not yet match the level of need.

Another important issue is transparency. There is limited public information on pricing, volumes, manufacturing capacity, and allocation. Without this information, it is difficult for governments, communities, and partners to fully understand the situation and contribute meaningfully to solutions. Transparency is not only a technical issue; it supports equitable participation and builds trust. 

This situation also resonates with ongoing global discussions on access and equity, including <a href='https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/pandemic-prevention--preparedness-and-response-accord?utm_source=chatgpt.com' target=_blank>negotiations around the Pandemic Agreement and Pathogen Access and Benefit Sharing</a> (PABS). These processes are similarly shaping how global systems are integrated to manage access to health technologies. The experience with lenacapavir highlights what is at stake. As procurement and supply pathways become more centralized, it becomes important to ensure that integration does not unintentionally limit flexibility or exclude certain actors and populations. Without clear, transparent, and inclusive mechanisms, there is a risk that access to critical innovations may remain uneven. These lessons are important as global frameworks are being shaped, as they will influence how future health emergencies and access to life-saving tools are managed.

This moment also highlights the importance of demand. Procurement volumes reflect supply realities, but also how demand is defined and prioritized. It is important that community perspectives inform demand forecasting, helping to reduce the gap between innovation and real-world impact.

The lesson is not to move away from integration, but to refine it. Integration should not replace independent and community-linked actors. Instead, it should recognize and preserve their roles within a coordinated system. Governments bring scale, donors bring financing, and centralized systems bring efficiency. Communities and independent actors bring reach, innovation, and accountability. A balanced approach is very much possible as integration can be strengthened with clear safeguards that ensure multiple delivery pathways remain open, especially for populations not well served by public systems. Procurement models can also allow some flexibility while maintaining coordination, and global strategies can include community voices in shaping both demand and delivery.

As the global health community advances new prevention tools like lenacapavir, the focus should remain on impact. Scientific breakthroughs alone are not enough. Delivery systems must be inclusive, responsive, and grounded in people’s realities. The MSF–Gilead case offers a useful reminder. Integration should not mean uniformity. In the case of HIV prevention, it should mean working together across levels and actors, while preserving the strengths that each brings to prevent new incidences. This is how innovation can translate into equitable access for everyone, everywhere.
